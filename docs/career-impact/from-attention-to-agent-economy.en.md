# From Attention Economy to Agent Economy: How Five Conversations Reframe the Business Model

**Core insight**: For twenty years the internet has rested on "Human browses": people search, compare, click, choose, and transact. Personal Agents introduce another mode: "Human delegates. Agent acts." The center of commercial competition may therefore add a second contest on top of the fight for human attention: the fight to be chosen by the Agent. This is more than "which apps will AI replace." It is a redistribution of the internet's commercial value chain: money may partly move from selling attention, selling seats, and charging intermediary fees toward transaction take rates, outcome-based pricing, and compute. If Agents compete enough with each other, some of it also flows back to consumers and suppliers as lower prices. And how an Agent gets paid will ultimately decide whom it represents.

## What I used to think vs. what I think now

**I used to think**: the Agent's impact on business happens mainly at the **front door** — companies need to add a "Choose me" layer (give the Agent a reason to pick me) on top of "Rank me" (get humans to see me), an upgrade to SEO and apps (see [From SEO to Agent Economy](from-seo-to-agent-economy.en.md)).

**Now I think**: the front door is only the first layer. The five conversations start from the perspectives of a founder, an assistant tester, an enterprise platform, a developer ecosystem, and an investor, yet they all point to the same thing: once the Agent becomes a person's **economic proxy** (Agent as Economic Proxy), the whole value chain gets repriced — interface, distribution, how transactions start, who owns the customer relationship, and what counts as scarce. And whom a proxy represents depends largely on **who pays it**.

## 01. Five conversations, one story

Recently I listened to five discussions on Personal AI Agents in a row:

### 1. Patrick O'Shaughnessy × Noah Shinn — Instinct

What Instinct founder Noah Shinn describes is no longer a traditional chatbot. It is a Personal Agent with its own phone and its own computer that can act on the user's behalf. Users text, call, or email it the way they would a person.

The business model matters even more.

Noah explicitly rejects advertising: a Personal Agent that represents the user's interests should not change its recommendations because an advertiser paid. In his framing, an AI that understands you better than anyone and then nudges you toward whoever pays most would be "an extremely sophisticated salesman disguised as your best friend." His alternative: **free for users for life, with merchants and services paying a take rate when transactions flow through the platform** (he compares it to Apple Pay). The episode notes Instinct is approaching $1 billion in annualized transaction volume, about half of it travel.

Instinct is also building a Trusted Network: users' Agents can coordinate directly — confirming mutual availability, putting meetings on calendars — with access levels set by relationship (a spouse can share everything; a colleague sees only what's granted).

**Key takeaway**: the Agent is starting to become a person's economic proxy, not just an information assistant.

### 2. a16z: Anish Acharya × David Pawlan — The Personal Agent Race Is Here

David Pawlan has spent a long time testing different Personal Agents. One important judgment from their conversation:

**The best Agents may become increasingly "invisible."**

It doesn't need to wait for the user to open an app each time. It can proactively check you in, find refunds, file reimbursements, manage email, and handle everyday admin.

The discussion extends to Agent-to-Agent interaction, and whether a large class of agent-native services will appear.

**Key takeaway**: software is moving from "waiting to be operated" to "getting it done for you."

### 3. Satya Nadella — the Agent market could be orders of magnitude bigger than Cloud

Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella argues that Agents could create a market "orders of magnitude" bigger than Cloud, calling it "probably the biggest TAM expansion ever."

The reason shouldn't be read simply as "AI software will sell more."

Cloud mainly monetizes compute, storage, and software usage; Agents could reach into large amounts of real economic activity itself — work, procurement, travel, customer service, sales, administrative processes, and software development.

Nadella also puts trust at the center of the Agent era: once software starts acting on people's behalf, reliability, security, and governance are no longer optional add-ons.

**Xiao Miao's addition**: two pieces of context. First, this came at the launch of the new Copilot and is mainly about the **enterprise** Agent market. Second, Microsoft sells both an Agent platform and Azure compute, so it gets paid whichever Agent wins; "the market will be huge" is a position as well as a forecast. The real meaning of "bigger than Cloud" is more likely that **the budget pool changes**: Cloud draws on companies' IT budgets; Agents aim at part of their labor budgets (see §08).

**Key takeaway**: the Agent TAM is not just software; it may extend to the execution layer of human economic activity.

### 4. Greg Isenberg — Muse Connectors: The Next App Store Moment?

Greg Isenberg compares Muse Connectors to the App Store moment of 2008.

Before:

```
User → App → Service
```

Increasingly:

```
User → Agent → Connector/API → Service
```

A [Connector](../../glossary.en.md#connector) means merchants and developers don't first have to show the user a beautiful UI; instead their capabilities can be discovered, understood, and called directly by the Agent.

But what really matters may be more than a new App Store.

The App Store decides: **which app the user sees.**

The Agent may go further and decide: **who the user's need is ultimately allocated to.**

So the Connector is the technical layer; the deeper economic question is: **Choose Me.**

### 5. The Synopsis: Drew × Mostly Borrowed Ideas

Of the five, this conversation makes the commercial impact most concrete.

MBI held Airbnb and is also a heavy Airbnb user. About ten days after Muse launched, he spotted a cottage on Airbnb and casually asked Muse whether he could book it directly. According to reports, Muse answered within a minute: yes, and booking direct would save about 60%; his credit card was already linked, so all he had to do was tap to confirm. The experience changed his view of how Agents would hit OTAs; he sold his Airbnb position and added to Meta.

**Xiao Miao's addition**: that 60% shouldn't be read as "the Agent cut out Airbnb's fee." Airbnb's service fees are nowhere near 60%, so other factors must be in the savings (the host's direct-booking pricing, dates, cleaning-fee structure, and so on). What it really shows is that **the Agent drove the cost of finding a direct-booking channel close to zero**. Hosts taking bookings off-platform has always existed; consumers just used to have to find those channels themselves.

The episode doesn't stop at Airbnb. It moves on to Booking, Uber, DoorDash, Amazon, and a concept well worth keeping:

**Proactive Commerce.**

This is not a conclusion about whether to buy or sell a particular stock. What matters more is the structural change it exposes:

Consumers used to have to enter a marketplace themselves and do the discovery, comparison, and transaction on their own.

An Agent can take over a substantial part of that work.

**Key takeaway**: the first to be repriced may be the aggregators built on lowering consumers' search, comparison, and coordination costs.

## 02. From sell-side optimization to buy-side optimization

Over twenty years, the internet built an extremely powerful seller-side machine:

```
Ads + SEO + Recommendation + CRM + Retargeting + Dynamic Pricing + Sales
```

Companies have ever more powerful tools for studying: **how do I get consumers to choose me?**

The consumer side's tools have been far weaker.

**Xiao Miao's revision**: saying "consumers only had themselves" isn't quite right. Buy-side tools have always existed: price-comparison sites, Google Flights price tracking, coupon extensions, consumer reviews. But they had two problems:

1. **Fragmentation**: each tool covers one step or one category; none knows your budget, calendar, memberships, and long-term goals all at once.
2. **Mostly funded by the sell side**: comparison sites and coupon extensions often earn mainly from merchant commissions. PayPal's Honey drew controversy in 2024 over allegations that it replaced creators' affiliate links and, through its merchant partnerships, didn't always show the best discount — nominally saving buyers money while earning from sellers.

So what's new about Personal Agents is not "the first buy-side tool," but possibly the first **integrated buy-side machine that can execute**:

**Buy-side Optimization Machine**

It knows the user's preferences, budget, history, calendar, loyalty memberships, credit cards, constraints, and long-term goals.

So it can, on the user's behalf:

```
Search → Compare → Negotiate → Decide → Pay → Monitor
```

Commercial power may start to move from one-sided to two-sided optimization. But the Honey lesson is that **whether a buy-side tool really sits on the buyer's side depends on where its money comes from** — the question §08 takes up.

## 03. Attention Economy → Intent Economy

The basic commercial chain of the old internet:

```
Attention → Traffic → Click → Conversion → Transaction → Retention
```

A company first had to win human attention.

The Agent era may gradually add another chain:

```
Intent → Delegation → Agent Selection → Execution → Transaction → Trust
```

The biggest change happens at the entry point. It used to be **Attention**; increasingly it may be **Intent**.

The consumer no longer needs to decide first whether to go to Google, Amazon, Booking, or some app. They just say:

> "I want a two-week vacation in Hawaii."

The rest is handed to the Agent.

That makes the **Intent Router** a potentially very important position in the Agent Economy:

Whoever decides which Connector, merchant, or service provider a user's intent gets routed to is shaping how commercial demand is allocated.

## 04. Apps may not disappear, but they will be repriced

Agents don't mean that apps, SaaS, or marketplaces all disappear.

What may really happen is that:

```
UI + Distribution + Workflow + Data + Transaction + Fulfillment
```

— value layers that used to be bundled into one product — get unbundled by the Agent.

Of these:

- the relative value of UI may fall;
- part of the workflow may be taken over by the Agent;
- distribution may gradually come under the Agent's influence;

while these capabilities may become more important:

```
Proprietary Data
Inventory
Fulfillment
Identity
Payments
Trust
Permissions
Governance
Physical Infrastructure
```

So the more accurate judgment is not: ~~Agent kills apps.~~

It is: **Agent reprices the software and commerce stack.**

## 05. Why aggregators deserve special attention

Airbnb, Booking, DoorDash, Uber, and Amazon are not the same kind of company; they have very different assets and moats.

So we can't simply conclude that "Agents will destroy platforms."

But Agents will force every platform to answer one question:

**Beyond discovery, search, comparison, and coordination, what irreplaceable value do you actually create?**

If the core value is mainly "I gather lots of suppliers so consumers can find them easily," the Agent may compress the value of that layer.

If the platform also has exclusive supply, logistics, fulfillment, insurance, payments, a credit/reputation system, proprietary data, or infrastructure, those capabilities may remain very valuable — and may even become infrastructure the Agent needs to call. There are early signs: Muse's announced commerce partners include an OTA like Expedia, which chose to become supply behind the Agent rather than only defend its own app.

Therefore:

**Agents may not destroy platforms. Agents will take platforms apart and re-judge what each layer is really worth.**

**Xiao Miao's addition: two questions Lao Jia didn't unpack**

**① When the platform is bypassed, who bears the risk?** Part of Airbnb's fee buys a backstop: guest protection, the review system, payment escrow, dispute resolution. Booking direct removes exactly that layer. If the place doesn't match its description, does responsibility fall on the user, the Agent platform, or the host? For an Agent to truly replace an aggregator, it has to absorb some of that risk itself — which would make Agent platforms look more like insurers and payment companies than just smart assistants.

**② Disintermediation, or a new intermediary?** Aggregators are powerful because they own demand; suppliers have to come to them for customers. If one Agent holds enough users' intent (§03's Intent Router), it becomes **a new aggregator** — a stronger one, since it even filters what the user gets to see. So the Agent Economy may be less disintermediation than **re-intermediation**: the middleman changes from Airbnb to the Agent platform. Whether consumers actually save money depends on whether Agent platforms compete enough, and whether users can easily take their preferences and history to a different Agent.

## 06. Proactive Commerce

Traditional commerce is reactive:

```
I have a need → open an app → search → compare → buy
```

Personal Agents make another mode possible:

```
Agent understands the goal → spots a need/opportunity → searches → compares → suggests or executes
```

For example:

- "If the fare drops, get me the credit."
- "When the detergent is running low, reorder it."
- "Tell me if you find a better insurance plan."
- "If a subscription raises its price, deal with it."

This means commerce may move from **Reactive Commerce** to **Proactive Commerce**.

What changes is not just the buying interface, but **who starts the commercial process once a need exists**.

**Xiao Miao's addition**: proactive commerce doesn't start from zero. Amazon's Subscribe & Save, Google Flights' price alerts, and credit-card price protection are early forms. But those were **single-category with preset rules**; what's different with Agents is that they work across categories, don't need the user to write rules in advance, and can execute directly.

There's also an incentive problem to watch: if the Agent earns a take rate per transaction, then "proactively starting a transaction" means **one more source of revenue** for it. "Saving you money" and "getting you to spend more" can come from the same feature. That's the point of §08: whether proactive commerce is good for users depends on how the Agent is paid.

## 07. The structural conflict between advertising and Personal Agents

A central business model of the traditional internet:

```
Advertiser pays platform → platform influences consumer choice.
```

But a Personal Agent that truly represents the user should optimize for: **User Utility**

That creates a built-in tension:

```
Advertiser pays Agent
        vs.
User trusts Agent
```

Noah Shinn's explicit rejection of advertising is an early sign of this problem.

**Xiao Miao's revision**: Noah isn't the only one rejecting ads. Meta — one of the world's largest advertising companies — has publicly said **Muse will not carry ads**: the basic version is free, heavy users have $20 / $100 monthly tiers, and long-term revenue comes from **charging merchants a small fee** on transactions Muse completes for users. Zuckerberg's reasoning: ads get paid regardless of what the user does afterward, while a transaction fee is only earned when Muse actually helps the user buy what they wanted, so it is better aligned with the user.

That matters far more than "a startup refuses ads": **the two most-watched consumer Personal Agents, starting from completely different places, converged on the same model — free for users, merchants pay per transaction, no ads.**

But that doesn't make the conflict disappear; it changes shape:

- A transaction take rate is essentially an **affiliate commission** model. If Hotel A pays 5% and Hotel B pays 12%, an Agent recommending B still can't clearly say whether it's for the user or the platform ([From SEO to Agent Economy §7](from-seo-to-agent-economy.en.md#7-advertising-what-if-agents-dont-look-at-ads) raised this).
- Take rates reward "more transactions, higher prices," not necessarily "happier users."
- A take rate is one step closer to the user's interest than ads, but not all the way. The real safeguard may lie less in how the Agent charges and more in disclosure, auditable recommendation logic, and the competitive pressure of users being able to switch Agents.

So a Personal Agent's most important asset may not be `MAU / Time Spent / Engagement`, but:

**Delegated Trust**

Whether users are willing to let it read their email, understand their calendar, remember long-term preferences, use their credit card, communicate on their behalf, and ultimately act for them.

## 08. Where the money goes: how Agents get paid (Xiao Miao's addition)

Lao Jia's framework makes clear how the value chain gets unbundled, but leaves one question unanswered head-on: **where does the money come from, and where does it go?**

### The ways an Agent can charge

| Model | Who pays | Existing examples | Aligned with the user? | Hard part |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Subscription** | User | Muse's $20 / $100 tiers | Mostly: the user is the only customer | Low ceiling; the more "invisible" the Agent, the harder users find it to perceive value and pay |
| **Advertising** | Advertiser | The old internet's main model; Muse and Instinct both explicitly reject it | Most conflicted | Structurally at odds with Delegated Trust (§07) |
| **Transaction take rate** | Merchant | Instinct (compared to Apple Pay), Muse (small fee charged to merchants) | Partly: paid only when a deal closes | Essentially affiliate commission; rewards "more deals, higher prices," not necessarily "satisfied users" |
| **Share of outcomes** | User | Refund, price-comparison, and bill-negotiation services that take a share of savings | Most aligned: paid only when it really saves the user money | Hard to measure whether the user would have found that money without the Agent |
| **Per task / per outcome (enterprise)** | Enterprise | Salesforce Agentforce initially priced per conversation; Intercom Fin prices per resolved ticket | Aligned with the enterprise customer | Cannibalizes the vendor's own per-seat revenue |

### Where the money moves from

The first to move may be merchants' **customer-acquisition budgets**: money that used to go to search ads, social ads, and platform commissions (what Airbnb, Booking, and DoorDash charge). In the Agent era, part of it may become transaction fees paid to Agent platforms.

On the enterprise side, this is what Nadella's "bigger than Cloud" corresponds to: Cloud draws on IT budgets; Agents aim at part of the **labor budget**. It also explains why SaaS will be forced to reprice: if one Agent does the work of ten seats, a per-seat vendor loses revenue to its own Agent and has to move to per-task or per-outcome pricing.

### Where the money goes

| Destination | Condition |
|---|---|
| **Agent platforms** (transaction fees, subscriptions) | The Agent holds enough users' intent, and users can't easily switch away |
| **Back to consumers and suppliers** (lower prices, higher take-home) | Agent platforms compete and take rates get pushed down; MBI's direct booking is this case |
| **The compute layer** (models, cloud, chips) | Holds whichever model wins: every act on someone's behalf consumes inference compute |
| **Stays with the old platforms** | The platform holds layers Agents can't route around: fulfillment, insurance, payments, exclusive supply (§05) |

The third row is the easiest to overlook. Instinct's "free for life" and Muse's "a huge number of free tokens" both assume **transaction fees can cover inference costs**. An Agent booking a flight may run dozens of searches, comparisons, and page actions behind the scenes, and each one costs money. Noah's discussion of the compute crunch from growing so fast shows this isn't theoretical. The most certain winner of the Agent Economy may be whoever sells compute.

### One judgment

> **How a proxy gets paid decides whom it represents.**

Human society answered this long ago: a real-estate agent paid a commission on the sale price naturally wants you to buy higher and close faster; a fee-only financial advisor who takes no product commissions is seen as more on the client's side. Personal Agents are the same. "Who represents the human?" ultimately has to be answered by **"Who pays the agent?"**

## 09. From Know Your Customer to Know My Human

Throughout the internet era, companies have worked hard to **Know Your Customer.** (Used here literally. In finance, KYC originally means identity verification and anti-money-laundering compliance when opening an account.)

- Google knows what users search for.
- Meta knows what users pay attention to.
- Amazon knows what users buy.

But a Personal Agent has the chance to occupy a completely different position: **Know My Human.**

It knows not only what a person has done, but may gradually understand what they want to do, why, their budget, what they dislike, how they've chosen before, and what really matters to them.

More importantly, it represents the user's side.

So what Personal Agents are ultimately competing for may not just be the `AI assistant market`, but:

**Who represents the human?**

## 10. Agent ↔ Agent: the next market layer

Once Personal Agents become people's proxies, the next step follows naturally: **Agent ↔ Agent**

A buyer Agent and a seller Agent can directly discover needs, request quotes, compare, negotiate, coordinate schedules, confirm permissions, and complete transactions.

At that point, the internet's basic unit may gradually shift from:

```
Page → App → Click
```

to:

```
Intent → Agent → Action → Transaction
```

And identity, payments, reputation, permissions, security, and governance become the infrastructure of this economic system.

**Xiao Miao's addition**: this infrastructure isn't speculative; people started laying it in 2025. Google proposed the A2A protocol for agent-to-agent communication and the AP2 protocol for agent payments; OpenAI and Stripe launched the Agentic Commerce Protocol along with Instant Checkout in ChatGPT (free for users, merchants pay on completed orders — the take-rate model again); Visa and Mastercard each launched payment programs for Agents. Instinct's Trusted Network is an early consumer-side example, currently used mainly for scheduling.

## 11. The final framework: five shifts plus one

The five conversations start from Personal Agents, consumer behavior, enterprise, the developer ecosystem, and investment/business models — yet they point to the same change:

| Layer | Shift | In one sentence |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Interface Shift** | App → Agent | People no longer need to operate every piece of software themselves |
| **2. Distribution Shift** | SEO / App Store → Connector / Agent Selection | Companies need not only humans to find them, but Agents to be able to discover, understand, trust, and call them |
| **3. Commerce Shift** | Reactive Commerce → Proactive Commerce | Agents start proactively finding and executing opportunities on the user's behalf |
| **4. Relationship Shift** | Merchant owns customer → Agent may own relationship | The merchant gets the transaction; the Personal Agent has a chance to hold the long-term relationship |
| **5. Economic Shift** | Attention Economy → Intent Economy | The scarce resource moves from "who gets human attention" to also include "who gets human delegation, and who gets chosen by the Agent" |
| **Addition: Monetization Shift** (Xiao Miao) | Selling attention / seats / intermediary fees → take rates / outcome pricing / compute | Money follows "who completed the action"; the pay structure decides whom the Agent represents |

For the first time, this framework strings together concepts we had discussed piecemeal: the Connector ([From SEO to Agent Economy](from-seo-to-agent-economy.en.md)), Choose Me (same), Trust ([From "Smartest" to "Most Trustworthy"](capability-to-trust.en.md)), the Intent Economy ([Personal Agents](personal-agents-agent-economy.en.md)), and the newly added Proactive Commerce. The sixth layer answers the question the first five leave open: once the value chain is unbundled, where does the money actually go?

## One last judgment

Whether Muse, Instinct, or today's other Personal Agents end up winning is not the most important question.

What deserves long-term attention is: **once software truly starts acting on people's behalf, what happens to the internet?**

The internet's basic assumption used to be: **Human browses.**

The Agent Economy's basic assumption may become: **Human delegates. Agent acts.**

If that change holds, what gets redesigned is not just the app, but distribution, advertising, commerce, payments, marketplaces, SaaS, and the relationship between companies and consumers.

> **The Connector is the technical layer.**
> **Choose Me is the distribution layer.**
> **Proactive Commerce is the transaction layer.**
> **Delegated Trust is the relationship layer.**
> **Agent as Economic Proxy is the deepest layer.**

**Xiao Miao's addition**: if the Agent is an economic proxy, the last question is the old answer to the principal–agent problem: **whoever pays it, it tends to represent.** Agent as Economic Proxy is the deepest layer, and whether that layer holds is decided by its pay structure.

## What We Know / What Is Emerging / What We Infer (Xiao Miao's addition)

Using the three-way split from [From SEO to Agent Economy](from-seo-to-agent-economy.en.md#what-we-know--what-is-emerging--what-we-infer), sorting this article's judgments by strength of evidence:

### What We Know — already happened

- Both major consumer Personal Agents (Muse, Instinct) are free or have a large free allowance, explicitly reject ads, and plan to **charge merchants transaction fees**.
- Instinct is approaching $1 billion in annualized transaction volume, about half travel; Muse's announced commerce partners include Walmart, Best Buy, Sephora, Expedia, Instacart, and others.
- Protocols for agent-to-agent communication and agent payments already exist (A2A, AP2, the Agentic Commerce Protocol, and Visa's and Mastercard's programs).
- On the enterprise side, Agent products priced per task or per outcome already exist.

### What Is Emerging — taking shape

- Investors are starting to re-evaluate platform companies by "exposure to Agents" (MBI is one public example).
- Some aggregators are choosing to become supply for Agents (Expedia joining Muse) rather than only defending their own apps.
- Agents finding direct-booking channels that bypass platforms is moving from an anecdote toward a repeatable experience.

### What We Infer — today's extrapolation

1. The mainstream Personal Agent business model may be "free for users + merchants pay transaction fees," pushing Agent platforms toward an affiliate-style incentive structure that needs disclosure and auditability as a counterweight.
2. Platforms that rely only on "aggregation + search and comparison" are hit hardest; those holding fulfillment, insurance, payments, or exclusive supply may become Agent infrastructure.
3. The Agent Economy is more likely re-intermediation than disintermediation; how much consumers get back depends on competition among Agent platforms and the cost of switching Agents.
4. SaaS pricing will shift from per seat to per task or per outcome.
5. The compute layer is the most certain beneficiary.

**A note on strength of evidence**: all five conversations fall in the September 2026 hype window after Muse's launch; MBI's judgment comes from roughly ten days of personal use, and the 60% saving is a single example; Nadella speaks from a platform owner's position. They are good **signals**, not yet **evidence**. Worth checking back in a few months.
