# How Discount Rates Determine Stock Valuations: The Interest Rate → Valuation Transmission

**Core concept**: A stock's price = the present value of its future cash flows; and "present value" requires a discount rate to translate future money into today's terms. The foundation of the discount rate is the risk-free rate (U.S. Treasury yield). So when interest rates move, the valuation bedrock of every stock shifts — this is why "Treasury yields" can sway the entire stock market, especially growth stocks.

---

## The Core Formula (Intuitive Version)

```
Stock Value ≈ Σ  Future cash flow per year / (1 + Discount Rate)^Years
```

- **Discount Rate = Risk-Free Rate (Treasury Yield) + Risk Premium**
- The `(1 + Discount Rate)^Years` in the denominator is the "discount factor": the further out in time and the higher the discount rate, the steeper the discount

---

## Three Key Implications

### 1. Interest Rates ↑ → Valuations ↓ (Inverse Relationship)

Higher discount rate → future cash flows are worth less in today's terms → stock prices should fall. Conversely, rates ↓ lifts valuations.

> Memory anchor: the interest rate is a stock's "denominator." A bigger denominator makes a smaller fraction.

### 2. The Longer a Stock's Duration, the More Sensitive It Is to Interest Rates

This is the most important one.

- **Growth stocks / unprofitable companies**: Most of their value comes from cash flows "many years from now" → the exponent in the discount factor is large → a small rate move causes dramatic swings in present value → **most sensitive to interest rates**
- **Value stocks / high-dividend mature companies**: Cash flows arrive "right now" → less affected by rate changes

> This explains why tech/AI stocks fall harder than utilities during a rate-hiking cycle — it's not that their fundamentals are worse, it's that their "duration" is longer. Analogy: the interest rate is gravity, and the more an asset "floats out in the future," the harder it gets pulled down.

### 3. Markets Trade on Expectations, Not the Present

What really drives yields isn't what the Fed did today, but **the market's expectations about what the Fed will do next**. So employment data, CPI, oil prices, and official statements — the moment they shift "expectations," yields move immediately, without waiting for an actual rate hike or cut.

---

## The Transmission Chain

```
Employment / Inflation / Oil price data + Fed commentary
        ↓ Shifts market expectations about the Fed
     Change in rate hike / cut probabilities
        ↓
   Treasury yields (risk-free rate) move
        ↓ Feeds into the discount rate (denominator)
   Stock valuations reprice — high-valuation growth stocks swing the most
```

---

## Common Points of Confusion

- **"Higher rates always means stocks fall" is wrong**: If earnings growth is fast enough, it can outpace the rising discount rate. The rate is just the denominator — the numerator (cash flows) is also changing
- **Nominal vs. real interest rates**: What truly affects valuations is closer to the real rate (nominal minus inflation expectations)
- **Rate hikes ≠ rate cuts cycle**: 2026 is unusual — the Fed is stuck on "whether to keep hiking," so the key phrase is "hiking expectations fading," not an assumption that cuts are coming
